Fluidized Beds

An interactive guide to gas–solid fluidization — from the Ergun equation to fluid catalytic cracking

Overview: What Is a Fluidized Bed?

Take a vertical column, fill it partway with solid particles — sand, catalyst beads, grains of coal — and blow a gas upward through it from below. At low gas velocity, the gas simply seeps through the gaps between the particles and nothing moves. Turn the gas up, and something remarkable happens: at a certain velocity the particles lift off their neighbours, separate, and the whole bed starts to churn and slosh around like a boiling liquid. That is fluidization, and the churning mixture of gas and solids is a fluidized bed.

Why fluidized beds matter

A fluidized bed mixes solids and gas almost perfectly. Every particle is surrounded by fresh gas, heat moves through the bed rapidly, and the temperature is nearly uniform everywhere. That combination is why chemical engineers use fluidized beds for:

  • Catalytic reactors — most famously fluid catalytic cracking (FCC), which turns heavy oil into gasoline and diesel (see the FCC tab).
  • Drying and coating of grains, powders, and pharmaceuticals.
  • Combustion and gasification of coal and biomass.
  • Heat treatment and roasting of ores.

The central question of this site: at what gas velocity does a bed fluidize? Answering it means combining the pressure drop through a packed bed (the Ergun equation) with a force balance on the bed itself — and that combination gives the minimum fluidization velocity, \(u_{mf}\), the single most important number in the subject.

Fixed Bed & the Ergun Equation

Before anything fluidizes, the bed is fixed: the particles rest on each other and on the distributor, and the gas flows through the voids between them. Pushing gas through those narrow, winding passages costs pressure — and the pressure drop is the first thing we need to predict.

The two quantities that describe the bed

\[ \varepsilon = \frac{\text{volume of voids}}{\text{total volume of bed}} \qquad u = \frac{Q}{A} \]
What they are used for: the voidage \(\varepsilon\) (typically 0.35–0.5 for a bed of spheres) says how much of the bed is empty space, and the superficial velocity \(u\) is the gas flow rate \(Q\) divided by the empty cross-sectional area \(A\) of the column — the velocity the gas would have if the column were empty. Every equation in this site is written in terms of \(u\) and \(\varepsilon\).

The Ergun equation

In 1952, Sabri Ergun combined two limiting behaviours into one equation for the pressure drop per unit length of bed, \(\Delta P / L\). It is the workhorse of this whole subject:

\[ \boxed{\frac{\Delta P}{L} = \frac{150\,\mu\,(1-\varepsilon)^2\,u}{\varepsilon^3 d_p^2} + \frac{1.75\,(1-\varepsilon)\,\rho_g\,u^2}{\varepsilon^3 d_p}} \]
What it is used for: predicting the pressure drop across a packed or fixed bed from the gas properties (\(\mu\), \(\rho_g\)), the particle size \(d_p\), the voidage \(\varepsilon\), and the superficial velocity \(u\). The first term is the viscous term; the second is the inertial term.

Where the two terms come from

Viscous term — \( \dfrac{150\,\mu\,(1-\varepsilon)^2\,u}{\varepsilon^3 d_p^2} \). At low velocity the flow through the voids is laminar, like flow through a bundle of tiny tortuous tubes. The pressure drop then behaves like the Hagen–Poiseuille law: proportional to viscosity \(\mu\) and to velocity \(u\), and inversely proportional to the square of the particle size \(d_p^2\) (smaller particles → narrower passages → much larger drop). This is the Kozeny–Carman form. It dominates for small particles and slow gas.

Inertial term — \( \dfrac{1.75\,(1-\varepsilon)\,\rho_g\,u^2}{\varepsilon^3 d_p} \). At higher velocity the gas has to accelerate, separate, and re-form its streamlines around every particle, and that form drag costs pressure proportional to \(\rho_g u^2\), like the drag on any bluff body. This is the Burke–Plummer form. It dominates for large particles and fast gas.

Derived or empirical? The forms of the two terms come from theory (laminar channel flow and form drag), but the numbers 150 and 1.75 are not derived — they were fitted by Ergun to experimental pressure-drop data. The Ergun equation is a semi-empirical correlation, and it is only as good as the data behind it. This distinction matters: on this site, every equation is labelled derived or empirical.

Fluidization & the Minimum Fluidization Velocity

As the gas velocity rises, the Ergun pressure drop rises with it — until the upward push of the gas on the particles exactly balances their weight. At that moment the particles are on the point of lifting, the bed is on the point of fluidizing, and the velocity is the minimum fluidization velocity, \(u_{mf}\).

Step 1 — the force balance at fluidization (derived)

Consider the whole bed as a free body. The gas pushes up on it with the pressure force \(\Delta P \cdot A\); gravity pulls the particles down, opposed by the buoyancy of the gas they displace. At the point of fluidization the upward pressure force just balances the net (buoyant) weight of the bed:

\[ \text{pressure force upward} = \text{buoyant weight of the bed} \]
Force balance on the entire bed at the point of incipient fluidization.
\[ \Delta P \cdot A = W_{\text{particles}} - F_{\text{buoyancy}} \]
Weight of the solid particles minus the buoyancy force on them.
\[ \Delta P \cdot A = (1-\varepsilon)\,A\,H\,\rho_s\,g \;-\; (1-\varepsilon)\,A\,H\,\rho_g\,g \]
The solid volume in the bed is \((1-\varepsilon)AH\); multiply by density and \(g\) for each force.
\[ \Delta P \cdot A = (1-\varepsilon)\,A\,H\,(\rho_s-\rho_g)\,g \]
Factor out the common terms.
\[ \boxed{\Delta P = (1-\varepsilon)\,(\rho_s-\rho_g)\,g\,H} \]
Divide by the area \(A\). This is the pressure drop across the bed at fluidization.
\[ \boxed{\Delta P_{mf} = (1-\varepsilon_{mf})(\rho_s-\rho_g)\,g\,H} \]
What it is used for: the pressure drop the bed exerts (and the gas must supply) at the moment of fluidization. Note it does not depend on velocity — once the bed is fluidized, the pressure drop stays at this value even as the gas velocity rises, because the bed simply expands to let the extra gas through. \(\varepsilon_{mf}\) is the voidage at incipient fluidization.

Step 2 — set the Ergun pressure drop equal to the bed weight (derived)

The Ergun equation gives the pressure drop the gas would produce at velocity \(u\); the force balance gives the pressure drop the bed requires to lift. At \(u = u_{mf}\) they are equal:

\[ \Delta P_{\text{Ergun}} = \Delta P_{\text{bed weight}} \qquad \text{at } u = u_{mf} \]
The defining condition for incipient fluidization.
\[ H\left[\frac{150\,\mu\,(1-\varepsilon)^2\,u_{mf}}{\varepsilon^3 d_p^2} + \frac{1.75\,(1-\varepsilon)\,\rho_g\,u_{mf}^2}{\varepsilon^3 d_p}\right] = (1-\varepsilon)(\rho_s-\rho_g)\,g\,H \]
Ergun \(\Delta P\) (pressure drop per length × bed height \(H\)) set equal to the force-balance result.
\[ \frac{150\,\mu\,(1-\varepsilon)^2\,u_{mf}}{\varepsilon^3 d_p^2} + \frac{1.75\,(1-\varepsilon)\,\rho_g\,u_{mf}^2}{\varepsilon^3 d_p} = (1-\varepsilon)(\rho_s-\rho_g)\,g \]
Divide both sides by \(H\).
\[ \underbrace{\frac{1.75\,(1-\varepsilon)\,\rho_g}{\varepsilon^3 d_p}}_{B}\,u_{mf}^2 + \underbrace{\frac{150\,\mu\,(1-\varepsilon)^2}{\varepsilon^3 d_p^2}}_{A}\,u_{mf} - \underbrace{(1-\varepsilon)(\rho_s-\rho_g)\,g}_{C} = 0 \]
Collect the coefficients of \(u_{mf}^2\), \(u_{mf}\), and the constant into \(A\), \(B\), \(C\).
\[ u_{mf} = \frac{-A + \sqrt{A^2 + 4BC}}{2B} \]
Quadratic formula; the positive root is the physical one (the negative root would mean downward flow).
\[ \boxed{u_{mf} = \frac{-A + \sqrt{A^2 + 4BC}}{2B}}, \quad \begin{cases} A = \dfrac{150\,\mu\,(1-\varepsilon)^2}{\varepsilon^3 d_p^2} \\[6pt] B = \dfrac{1.75\,(1-\varepsilon)\,\rho_g}{\varepsilon^3 d_p} \\[6pt] C = (1-\varepsilon)(\rho_s-\rho_g)\,g \end{cases} \]
The full solution for the minimum fluidization velocity. Every quantity in it is a measurable property.

Step 3 — the small-particle closed form (derived)

For small particles the flow is slow and the viscous term dominates, so the inertial term (\(B u_{mf}^2\)) can be dropped. The quadratic collapses to a single closed-form answer:

\[ A\,u_{mf} = C \qquad \text{(inertial term neglected)} \]
Valid when the particle Reynolds number is small (roughly \(Re_p < 20\)).
\[ u_{mf} = \frac{C}{A} = \frac{(1-\varepsilon)(\rho_s-\rho_g)\,g\;\varepsilon^3 d_p^2}{150\,\mu\,(1-\varepsilon)^2} \]
Substitute \(A\) and \(C\) and divide.
\[ \boxed{u_{mf} = \frac{d_p^2\,(\rho_s-\rho_g)\,g\,\varepsilon_{mf}^3}{150\,\mu\,(1-\varepsilon_{mf})}} \qquad (Re_p < 20) \]
Cancel one factor of \((1-\varepsilon)\). This is the form most often used for fine powders and catalyst particles.

Step 4 — the Wen–Yu correlation (empirical cross-check)

Wen and Yu (1966) fitted a large body of fluidization data into a compact correlation. It is not derived — it is a curve fit — but it is the standard quick check against the Ergun result:

\[ \text{Ar} = \frac{d_p^3\,\rho_g\,(\rho_s-\rho_g)\,g}{\mu^2}, \qquad Re_{mf} = \sqrt{33.7^2 + 0.0408\,\text{Ar}} - 33.7, \qquad \boxed{u_{mf} = \frac{Re_{mf}\,\mu}{d_p\,\rho_g}} \]
What it is used for: a fast, independent estimate of \(u_{mf}\) from the Archimedes number \(\text{Ar}\), which combines particle size, densities, and gas viscosity into one dimensionless group. If the Ergun quadratic and Wen–Yu agree within ~10–20%, the estimate is trustworthy.

Derived vs. empirical, in one place. The force balance and the algebra that turns Ergun + force balance into \(u_{mf}\) are derived. The Ergun constants 150 and 1.75, and the Wen–Yu correlation, are empirical — fitted to experiments. A good engineer knows which parts of a calculation are exact and which are fitted.

Flow Regimes & the Geldart Classification

Fluidization is not one state — it is a progression. As the gas velocity rises from zero, the bed passes through a sequence of regimes, and the useful operating window for most processes sits between two velocities: the minimum fluidization velocity \(u_{mf}\) and the terminal velocity of a single particle \(u_t\).

Terminal velocity: the top of the operating window (derived)

A single particle falling (or being carried) through gas reaches a terminal velocity \(u_t\) when the drag on it exactly balances its buoyant weight. The operating window for a bubbling bed is \(u_{mf} < u < u_t\): below \(u_{mf}\) nothing moves, above \(u_t\) the gas carries the particles out of the column.

\[ F_D = F_g - F_b \]
Force balance on one particle at its terminal velocity.
\[ C_D\,\tfrac{1}{2}\rho_g u_t^2\,\tfrac{\pi d_p^2}{4} = \tfrac{\pi d_p^3}{6}\,(\rho_s-\rho_g)\,g \]
Drag (left) equals buoyant weight (right). \(\tfrac{\pi d_p^2}{4}\) is the projected area; \(\tfrac{\pi d_p^3}{6}\) is the particle volume.
\[ u_t^2 = \frac{4\,d_p\,(\rho_s-\rho_g)\,g}{3\,\rho_g\,C_D} \]
Cancel \(\pi\) and \(d_p^2\) and solve for \(u_t^2\).
\[ C_D = \frac{24}{Re}\left(1 + 0.15\,Re^{0.687}\right) \;\; (Re < 1000), \qquad C_D = 0.44 \;\; (Re \ge 1000), \qquad Re = \frac{\rho_g u_t d_p}{\mu} \]
Schiller–Naumann drag correlation (empirical). Because \(C_D\) depends on \(u_t\), the equation is solved iteratively.
\[ \text{Stokes regime } (Re < 1): \qquad \boxed{u_t = \frac{d_p^2\,(\rho_s-\rho_g)\,g}{18\,\mu}} \]
For very small particles \(C_D = 24/Re\); substituting gives the closed-form Stokes settling velocity.
\[ \boxed{u_{mf} < u < u_t} \]
What it is used for: the operating window of a bubbling fluidized bed. Stay inside it and the bed fluidizes without losing particles; cross \(u_t\) and the bed empties into the exit (that is pneumatic transport, not fluidization).

The Geldart classification

Not all powders fluidize equally. In 1973, Derek Geldart showed that particles fall into four groups according to their size and density difference with the gas. The group tells you at a glance how a powder will behave:

GroupTypical sizeBehaviourExample
A — aeratable30–100 μm, lightFluidizes smoothly; bed expands a lot before bubbling; bubbles split easilyFCC catalyst
B — sand-like100–1000 μmBubbles as soon as it fluidizes; the classic bubbling bedSand
C — cohesive< 30 μmSticky interparticle forces dominate; channels and plugs instead of fluidizingFine flour, soot
D — spoutablelarge and/or denseNeeds high velocity; tends to spout rather than bubbleCoarse coal, gravel

Bed Expansion & Entrainment

Once a bed is fluidized, extra gas does not raise the pressure drop — it makes the bed taller. The particles spread apart, the voidage rises, and the bed expands until the extra gas can pass through. Push the velocity past the terminal velocity and the gas starts carrying particles out of the column: that is entrainment.

The Richardson–Zaki relation (empirical)

Richardson and Zaki (1954) found that the voidage of a fluidized bed follows a simple power law in the velocity ratio:

\[ \boxed{\frac{u}{u_t} = \varepsilon^{\,n}} \]
What it is used for: predicting the bed voidage \(\varepsilon\) at any operating velocity \(u\), from the terminal velocity \(u_t\) and the exponent \(n\). The exponent depends on the terminal Reynolds number \(Re_t = \rho_g u_t d_p / \mu\): \(n = 4.65\) for \(Re_t < 0.2\); \(n = 4.4\,Re_t^{-0.03}\) for \(0.2 < Re_t < 1\); \(n = 4.4\,Re_t^{-0.1}\) for \(1 < Re_t < 500\); \(n = 2.4\) for \(Re_t > 500\). The values of \(n\) are fitted to experiments — empirical.

Bed height from a solids balance (derived)

The amount of solid in the bed does not change as it expands — the same particles just occupy more space. Equating the solid volume before and after expansion gives the expanded height:

\[ (1-\varepsilon_{mf})\,A\,H_{mf} = (1-\varepsilon)\,A\,H \]
Solid volume is conserved: the bed at minimum fluidization (height \(H_{mf}\), voidage \(\varepsilon_{mf}\)) and the expanded bed (height \(H\), voidage \(\varepsilon\)) contain the same particles.
\[ \boxed{H = H_{mf}\,\frac{1-\varepsilon_{mf}}{1-\varepsilon}} \qquad \text{with} \qquad \varepsilon = \left(\frac{u}{u_t}\right)^{1/n} \]
Cancel \(A\) and solve for \(H\). The voidage comes from Richardson–Zaki.

Entrainment: when \(u > u_t\)

Above the terminal velocity, the drag on a particle exceeds its buoyant weight and the gas carries it upward out of the bed. In a bubbling bed this happens first to the finest particles, which are thrown into the freeboard above the bed surface. Industrial units deal with this in two ways:

  • Freeboard and disengaging space — extra height above the bed so entrained particles slow and fall back.
  • Cyclones — the gas leaving the top is spun through a cyclone that throws the particles to the wall and returns them to the bed.

Entrainment is a loss when it is unwanted — and the whole point of a circulating fluidized bed, like the FCC riser in the next tab, where the particles are deliberately carried along with the gas and then separated and returned.

FCC: Fluid Catalytic Cracking

Fluid catalytic cracking (FCC) is the largest industrial use of fluidization — and the reason a fluid mechanics course cares about it. An FCC unit is a circulating fluidized bed: tiny catalyst particles are suspended in hot hydrocarbon vapour, carried up a tall pipe called a riser, separated, regenerated, and circulated back, tens of tonnes per minute. Its job is to take heavy gas oil — big, low-value molecules from crude oil distillation — and crack them into lighter, more valuable fuels.

Click a part of the diagram above to learn what happens there.

Why FCC works: the fluidization connection

FCC catalyst is a fine powder of zeolite crystals on an alumina-silica support, typically around 75 μm in diameter — squarely in Geldart Group A, the group that fluidizes most smoothly and expands the most before bubbling. That is no accident: a Group A powder gives the intimate gas–solid contact and rapid circulation the process needs, with contact times of just a few seconds in the riser. The riser itself is a transport (fast fluidization) regime device — the gas velocity is above the terminal velocity of the catalyst, so the particles are deliberately carried along with the vapour and then separated in cyclones at the top.

What comes out: the product slate

The cracked vapours are separated in the fractionator into a slate of products. The economics of the unit are driven by the light, high-value end:

ProductWhat it isTypical share*
GasolineHigh-octane motor fuel — the main product and the reason the unit exists≈ 45–55%
LPGPropane and butanes, including feed for alkylation and petrochemicals≈ 15–25%
Light cycle oil (LCO)Diesel-range material, blended into the diesel pool≈ 15–20%
Heavy cycle oil (HCO)Heavier material, often recycled to the riser for another pass≈ 5–10%
CokeCarbon laid down on the catalyst; burned off in the regenerator to supply the heat of cracking≈ 4–6%

*Shares vary with feed, catalyst, and operating severity; these are representative values.

Gasoline vs. diesel. FCC is tuned to maximise gasoline; the diesel-range cut (light cycle oil) is a secondary product. That is why the two fuels come up together in any discussion of FCC: the unit's job is to shift heavy oil toward the light transport fuels, and the gasoline/diesel split is the economic question a refinery answers every day.

How much of this project is FCC?

Deliberately about a fifth of it. The core of this site is the fluid mechanics of fluidization — the Ergun equation, \(u_{mf}\), the regimes, the Geldart groups, and bed expansion. FCC is the industrial payoff: it shows those ideas operating at full scale, and it is the reason fluidization is a core chemical engineering topic rather than a laboratory curiosity.

About This AI Project

This website is an AI project exploring AI capabilities: it was designed and built with the help of AI models, and one of the goals of the course project is to study that process itself — what the models did well, where they needed guidance, and how the results compare across models.

How version 1 was built

  • AI model: Muse (Meta), acting as the assistant “Perry” in a supervised build session.
  • Physics engine: written in AssemblyScript and compiled to WebAssembly (build/release.wasm). Every number on this site — the Ergun pressure drop, \(u_{mf}\), terminal velocity, Geldart group, and bed expansion — is computed by that module in the browser, not hard-coded.
  • User interface: plain HTML, CSS, and JavaScript; equations rendered with KaTeX; graphs and diagrams drawn on canvas and SVG.
  • Hosting: GitHub Pages, from the Brock-ChemE repository (see the README in the repository for the publish steps).
  • Human guidance: the group chose the topic, the dark academic style with University of Cincinnati colours, the eight-tab structure, which sections to include, that derivations should show every step, that no static worked numerical example should be included, and which interactive widgets to build. The AI proposed the plan and built the site; the group directed the decisions.

What I learned / what I want to learn more of

DRAFT — for the group to personalize. Working on this project showed how a single force balance and one empirical correlation (the Ergun equation) combine into a practical design number, the minimum fluidization velocity, that decides whether an entire industrial reactor works. The most surprising part was how much of fluidization engineering is empirical — the Ergun constants, the Wen–Yu correlation, and the Geldart groups are all fitted to experiments rather than derived. In future work, I want to learn more about how bubbling-bed models (such as the two-phase theory of Davidson) predict gas bypassing and conversion, and how circulating fluidized beds are scaled up from laboratory to refinery size.
